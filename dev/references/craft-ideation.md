# Ideation and Writing for Short Films: Craft Digest

This digest covers how short films are conceived and written: loglines and premises, thesis-driven essay films, music-video treatments, divergent concept methods and how to score them against a brief, tone arcs, on-screen text (reading speed and word budgets for captions and kinetic type), title-sequence practice, kinetic typography, scripts where the picture carries the meaning, and pitching for sign-off. The skill's ideation and storyboard phases should use the bullets under "What this means for the skill" as defaults that a brief can override. Figures marked secondary or unverified came from search excerpts or third-party pages, not from the primary document, and the bullets say so. Arithmetic done by the author of this digest, rather than stated by a source, is labelled as such.

## Sources

1. **Anatomy of a Murder (title sequence), Saul Bass, 1959.** Art of the Title, https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/anatomy-of-a-murder (page undated; film 1959).
   Bass built the sequence on one visual idea: the body, shown whole and then taken apart like a puzzle, in paper cut-outs on a plain grey ground, with no advanced technology needed.
   Confidence: high.

2. **Celebrating Saul Bass's centenary: 10 essential title sequences.** BFI, centenary list (c. 2020; the page shows no date).
   https://www.bfi.org.uk/lists/celebrating-saul-basss-centenary-10-essential-title-sequences
   Each entry says what the sequence does for its film's mood: Psycho's rushing bars and fractured type for a fragmented mind; West Side Story's credits as a decompression before the film; Vertigo's spirals as "a climate for the story".
   Confidence: high.

3. **From the archives: an interview with Kyle Cooper.** Revert to Saved, 2009. https://reverttosaved.com/2009/03/26/from-the-archives-an-interview-with-kyle-cooper/
   Titles should grow from the film's content and serve its characters; the director is the gatekeeper and the designer's job is to match the director's point of view. Cooper avoids carrying a signature style from film to film, designs in the edit room frame by frame, and prefers to make things in camera or by hand. The interview has no Se7en-specific detail.
   Confidence: high for the general method; the page was read as a summary.

4. **Kyle Cooper.** Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyle_Cooper (read in this pass; date of access not recorded).
   Cooper co-founded Imaginary Forces in 1996 and founded Prologue in 2003. Se7en (1995) was built largely by hand, with tabletop photography and hand-etched type. The article lists his main-title work, including Mission: Impossible, Spider-Man and Minority Report.
   Confidence: medium (Wikipedia; check dates against studio pages before quoting).

5. **Pacific Rim (main and end titles).** Art of the Title, https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/pacific-rim (film 2013; studio Imaginary Forces).
   The studio's first round was five distinct directions, each with four styleframes plus a few extra explorations. One extra, a wildcard from the director's interest, became the main-title reveal. The director then art-directed the chosen direction, asking for a brighter look and more reflectiveness.
   Confidence: medium-high (compiled from interviews on the Art of the Title page).

6. **Semi-Permanent Sydney 2019 (titles by Elastic).** Art of the Title, https://www.artofthetitle.com/title/semi-permanent-sydney-2019
   The designer pitched the project to the festival herself, with a co-director. The studio's clients "expect a certain look", so off-house-style pitches are often rejected or pushed back toward the studio look. The opening was deliberately dark and familiar before it departed from the studio style. The brief was a single theme word; the work took about eight months on a $50,000 budget.
   Confidence: medium-high (practitioner interview, paraphrased).

7. **Timed Text Style Guide: General Requirements.** Netflix Partner Help, https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617 (changelog dated 2020-07-24).
   The minimum duration per subtitle event is 5/6 of a second (20 frames at 24 fps) and the maximum is 7 seconds. Two lines is the default. Reading-speed limits are not on this page; they are set in each language's own guide, which applies on top of the general rules.
   Confidence: high.

8. **Captioning Key: Presentation Rate.** DCMP (Described and Captioned Media Program), https://dcmp.org/learn/captioningkey/601
   Presentation rate counts words, not characters. Targets are 130 words per minute for lower-level, 140 for middle-level and 160 for upper-level media. The targets give way to preserving meaning and essential vocabulary; quotes and sung lyrics are captioned verbatim. The page suggests shifting captions up to 15 frames as one way to meet the rate.
   Confidence: high (educational media target, not a broadcast or streaming standard).

9. **Viewers can keep up with fast subtitles: Evidence from eye movements.** Szarkowska and Gerber-Moron (2018), PLOS ONE 13(6): e0199331, https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0199331
   Eye-tracking study with 74 participants at 12, 16 and 20 characters per second (cps). Comprehension did not differ by speed, but slow captions drew more re-reading and frustration. The authors treat the six-second rule (about 140 to 150 words per minute, or 12 cps) as unnecessarily long for modern viewers, and report streaming services at 17 to 20 cps. The paper assumes an average word of five characters.
   Confidence: high.

10. **BBC Subtitling Guidelines, as summarised by Clevercast.** Secondary summary, https://clevercast.com/bbc-subtitling-guidelines (the BBC's own page could not be fetched in this pass).
    The summary gives 160 to 180 words per minute (about 0.33 to 0.375 seconds per word), two lines maximum (three only if no important picture information is hidden), and line width of 68 percent of a 16:9 frame. The sources disagree on the gap rule: one summary gives a 1.5-second minimum gap, while another says the BBC sets no frame gap and only requires a one-second pause in speech.
    Confidence: low (secondary; check the BBC guide's current version before quoting).

11. **So Much to Read, So Little Time: How Do We Read, and Can Speed Reading Help?** Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman (2016), Psychological Science in the Public Interest, with a commentary by Balota. https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/speed_reading.html
    Claims that readers can double or triple speed and keep full comprehension are overstated. Presenting text one word at a time (RSVP) loses comprehension for longer passages, because the eyes' regressions carry them back to the parts that need processing. Skimming for the gist is a legitimate mode, not a failure.
    Confidence: high.

12. **The emotional arcs of stories are dominated by six basic shapes.** Reagan, Mitchell, Kiley, Danforth and Dodds (2016), EPJ Data Science (preprint arXiv:1606.07772), https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.07772
    The team analysed the emotional trajectories of 1,327 Project Gutenberg fiction works (the arXiv abstract page gives this count; the earlier preprint and the published version differ in count and method wording) and found six core arcs as building blocks of more complex curves. The download-popularity claim rests on a thinner comparison than the arcs themselves.
    Confidence: medium.

13. **Kurt Vonnegut: the shapes of stories.** Farnam Street (fs.blog), 2011, https://fs.blog/2011/09/kurt-vonnegut-the-shapes-of-stories/
    The vertical axis runs from ill fortune to good fortune. Man in Hole starts at a bearable baseline, falls into trouble and ends a little higher. Vonnegut's reason is that readers want reassurance, and that a protagonist who ends higher is encouraging. The page covers only the Man in Hole shape; the other curves are not described.
    Confidence: medium (secondary account of a lecture).

14. **The Essay Film: From Montaigne, After Marker.** Timothy Corrigan (Oxford University Press, 2011), read through The Cine-Files' report of his talk at the Savannah Film Festival (2012). https://www.thecine-files.com/past-issues/2012-savannah-film-festival/chris-marker-and-the-essay-film-a-presentation-by-timothy-corrigan
    Corrigan describes the essay film as thinking out loud, where a shifting subjectivity meets public experience. He places its real start after 1945. Resnais's Night and Fog replaces the authoritative "voice of God" narrator with a survivor's questioning voice-over. Sans Soleil mixes travelogue, diary, news and critique.
    Confidence: medium (book not read directly; talk report read).

15. **The essay film: problems, definitions, textual commitments.** Laura Rascaroli, in Nora M. Alter and Timothy Corrigan (eds.), Essays on the Essay Film (2017), pp. 183 to 196 (library record: https://search.ub.tum.de/vufind/Record/DE-604.BV044440677). Her 2015 Sussex talk abstract: https://reframe.sussex.ac.uk/conversations/talksmfm/rascaroli-2015/
    The category is widely used but under-theorised, and the form is hybrid. Essay films display the process of thinking; their specificity lies in how images are arranged, and that arrangement rests on a basic structure of gap.
    Confidence: low-medium (library record and abstracts only; chapter text not read).

16. **6 Rules for Good Cutting According to Oscar-Winning Editor Walter Murch.** No Film School (2016), summarising the Rule of Six in Murch's In the Blink of an Eye, https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch
    Murch ranks six criteria: emotion, story, rhythm, eye trace, the two-dimensional plane of the screen, and three-dimensional space. Emotion carries the best-known weight, 51 percent. The article gives no figures for the other five; secondary write-ups give 23, 10, 7, 5 and 4 percent, which I did not verify against the book. The order itself is the useful part: when cuts conflict, give up the lower-ranked criteria first.
    Confidence: medium.

17. **Controlled Convergence.** Institute for Manufacturing, University of Cambridge (after Stuart Pugh), https://www.ifm.eng.cam.ac.uk/research/dmg/tools-and-techniques/controlled-convergence/
    Sketch 5 to 10 front-runners; agree about 10 criteria with the team; choose a datum; rate each concept against it as better (+), the same (S) or worse (-). A concept's score is pluses minus minuses. Combine and improve, then repeat with the strongest concept as the new datum and more specific criteria. Finish by checking whether one solution stands out.
    Confidence: high.

18. **Pugh matrix (concept selection), Session 5 slides.** MIT OpenCourseWare 16.842 (Fundamentals of Systems Engineering), Fall 2015, https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-842-fundamentals-of-systems-engineering-fall-2015/dac225aff5a84125fd1bf9364d748221_MIT16_842F15_Ses_5_Design.pdf
    Criteria should trace back to the requirements; the datum choice shapes the result; the method should not be the only means of selection. The course assignment reruns the matrix after rejecting the weakest concepts and adding hybrids. A Springer paper on the method (search excerpt) and a vendor page were also seen, not read in full.
    Confidence: medium (search excerpts of slides; full text not read).

19. **Framework for Innovation (the Double Diamond).** Design Council (UK), https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/framework-for-innovation/
    Four phases: Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver. The first diamond widens to understand the problem and then narrows to define it. The second widens to generate varied answers and narrows through small-scale trials that reject what will not work. The process is not linear. The page dates the model to 2004.
    Confidence: high.

20. **Design sprint.** Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Design_sprint
    A time-boxed process developed at Google Ventures from about 2010 to 2012, with Jake Knapp's early iterations; popularised by the 2016 book Sprint. The article describes five phases (Understand, Diverge, Converge, Prototype, Test). It does not mention Crazy Eights.
    Confidence: medium.

21. **Crazy 8s canvas.** Wade Institute, "The Studio" (a third-party adaptation of the Sprint exercise), https://studio.wadeinstitute.org.au/canvas-crazy-8s.html
    Set a timer for eight minutes; one idea per panel, sketched or written; a hard stop when the timer ends. Then look for patterns, pick the top idea and name the assumption under it. The page warns against spending four minutes on the first idea and says ideas 5 to 8 should differ noticeably from 1 to 4.
    Confidence: medium-low (adaptation; not the book's text).

22. **Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days.** Jake Knapp, John Zeratsky and Braden Kowitz (2016); publisher listing at https://www2.publishersweekly.com/9781501121746
    The book is the main public account of the sprint, with Crazy Eights as one of its exercises. The exact wording of its rules was not checked against the book.
    Confidence: medium (listing only; book not read).

23. **The three concept rule.** ZURB (design agency blog), https://zurb.com/blog/the-three-concept-rule
    Present three focused concepts rather than one or a long menu. Frame each around the problem it solves. A long menu invites the client to choose whatever looks best that day. The article argues that showing process builds trust.
    Confidence: medium (practitioner opinion).

24. **How long is a film treatment?** Robin Piree (director's blog), https://robinpiree.com/blog/how-long-is-a-film-treatment
    The author says there is no industry standard for treatment length. His own treatments run 20 to 30 pages, with large imagery and little text per slide. His size bands (1 to 10, 10 to 20, 20+ pages) are opinion. Follow the client's length instruction, and make sure the treatment covers the essential details, sells the vision and shows a plan.
    Confidence: medium-low (single director's opinion).

25. **6 steps to writing powerful loglines.** Frame.io blog (2016), https://blog.frame.io/2016/01/18/6-steps-to-writing-powerful-loglines/
    Two starting templates: protagonist's goal with an obstacle and a strategy; inciting event with the protagonist's objective and the stakes. Lead with what is distinctive. Say the logline aloud. Provoke curiosity. Cut extraneous detail. Write about ten more versions.
    Confidence: medium.

26. **Save the Cat!** Blake Snyder (2005), as summarised on Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Save_the_Cat!:_The_Last_Book_on_Screenwriting_You%27ll_Ever_Need
    A logline is one or two sentences that captures tone, dilemma, characters and audience. Snyder's stated requirements: an ironic or emotionally engaging hook, a mental picture of the whole film, a defined audience and budget, and a title that carries the story's substance. The "When..." template that some guides cite was not confirmed in any source in this pass. The Save the Cat homepage (savethecat.com) describes a 15-beat structure and no logline formula.
    Confidence: medium.

27. **5 Tips for Engaging Short Films.** Videomaker (2015), https://www.videomaker.com/videonews/2015/01/5-tips-for-engaging-short-films/
    Do not scale a feature's three acts down to a short. Hook at once, even in medias res. Build characters first and cut what does not develop them. Use non-linear time and held-back detail. Prioritise visuals; keep dialogue brief and make every shot carry story content. No numbers are given.
    Confidence: medium-low (magazine tips, no data).

28. **85 percent of Facebook video is watched without sound.** Digiday (May 17, 2016), https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/
    Publishers reported 85 percent silent views (LittleThings, Mic) and PopSugar 50 to 80 percent. The figures are self-reported and unaudited. Facebook counts a view at three seconds, so the opening seconds must hold attention.
    Confidence: low (2016 publisher self-reports; use as a directional reason to design for sound-off, not as a benchmark).

29. **Understanding SC 2.3.1: Three Flashes or Below Threshold.** W3C, WCAG 2.2, https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html
    Content must not contain more than three general flashes or red flashes in any one-second period, or must pass the area test. A general flash is an opposing luminance change of at least 10 percent of maximum where the darker state is below 0.80. For SDR, a change of 20 cd/m² or more counts. A red flash involves saturated red, with R/(R+G+B) of at least 0.8 on one state and a chromaticity difference above 0.2.
    Confidence: high.

30. **Impact of Kinetic Typography on Readers' Attention.** Kuraitytė, Bessemans and Nuyts, Visible Language 54 (2020), https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/4632
    Eye-tracking study comparing kinetic typography sub-categories with serial presentation. Attention duration differed significantly between fluid typography and serial presentation. The abstract page gives no values or direction, and does not measure comprehension directly.
    Confidence: medium-low.

31. **Temporal Typography: The Impact of Animation Speed and Interpolation on Information Transmission Efficiency.** Dolić, Pibernik, Muftić and Mandić, GRID 2024, https://www.grid.uns.ac.rs/symposium/download/2024/89.pdf
    The closest match to a study of on-screen word speed against comprehension. It varies words per minute and the interpolation method. The findings could not be extracted (the PDF was not readable in this pass), so no optimal rate is cited from it.
    Confidence: low.

32. **Safe area (television).** Wikipedia, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_area_(television), which cites EBU Recommendation R 95, "Safe areas for 16:9 television production" (https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r095.pdf; the PDF was not readable in this pass).
    R 95 separates an action-safe area for essential picture from a graphics-safe area for text and graphics. The margins seen in a search excerpt (3.5 percent action, 5 percent graphics, per edge) are not verified against the PDF. The Wikipedia article gives no figures.
    Confidence: medium for the concept; low for the numbers.

## What this means for the skill

- Ideate in two passes: diverge with a short burst of many quick options (about eight per brief), then converge on a shortlist. Do not start with the first idea that fits. [19][21]
- Run a Crazy Eights round when the brief is open: eight panels, one minute each, a hard stop. Make the last four differ from the first four, then look for patterns and name the assumption under the top pick. [21][22]
- Pitch three concepts, not one and not ten. Frame each around the problem it solves, not the look. [23]
- Internal exploration can be larger than the external pitch, and should keep one wildcard slot. One studio's round was five directions with about four styleframes each (a single example, not a standard); on Pacific Rim a wildcard the director was drawn to became the main title. Send only the shortlist out. [5]
- Score the shortlist with a Pugh-style matrix: 5 to 10 front-runners; about 10 criteria agreed with the director or client and drawn from the brief's promises (emotion, story, feasibility, budget, music fit, on-screen text load, rights); a datum (the strongest concept or the current benchmark); each concept marked +, S or -; rank by pluses minus minuses. Refine the top two and rerun once with stricter criteria. Treat totals as guidance, not a decision: rerun with a different datum to check the ranking holds, since the datum can change the order. [17][18]
- When criteria conflict on a cut or a concept, use Murch's order as the default tiebreak: emotion, story, rhythm, eye trace, screen plane, depth. Only the 51 percent emotion weight was seen in a fetched source; the other weights are unverified. [16]
- Before developing, ask the director or client for a keep-and-kill list. Cooper's account treats the director as the gatekeeper, so design from the film's own research and content, not a house style. [3]
- When a pitch departs from the look the client expects, bridge to that look first. Off-house-style pitches are often rejected, and Elastic's 2019 opening was deliberately familiar before it changed. [6]
- Write the logline before the treatment: one or two sentences giving tone, protagonist, dilemma, stakes and audience. Use the two Frame.io templates as starting points only, write about ten versions and read each one aloud. Test it by asking whether a reader can picture the whole film; Snyder's criteria add an irony or emotional hook and a title that carries the substance. [25][26]
- Default treatment for a two-minute music or essay film (the skill's default; no source sets a standard): three to five pages with a logline, a one-paragraph concept, a section-by-section walk against the track's structure (what happens in each verse, chorus or beat), style and reference frames, and a production note. Keep prose short and imagery large. [24]
- For an essay film, put the thesis in the arrangement of images and the gaps between them, not only in narration. A first-person or explicitly thinking voice is the form's signature. [14][15]
- Where an essay film argues, prefer a questioning narrator to an authoritative one, following Night and Fog. [14]
- Map the tone arc before the shot list: a baseline, a dip and a finish. Decide deliberately whether the film ends higher (Vonnegut's reassurance) or lower (to stay with dread), and write down why. [13]
- Use Reagan et al.'s six arcs as a diagnostic, not a template: check whether the emotional curve has a clear shape, and if the middle is flat, add a turn. [12]
- Structure a short as a compressed three-act story with a hook in the first seconds, not a scaled-down feature. Spend the runtime on the protagonist's dilemma and show it rather than explain it. [27]
- Build a title sequence on one strong visual idea that stands for the film's theme, and let it set the mood. Bass's body taken apart like a puzzle, in paper cut-outs on grey, shows how little is needed; his Psycho titles used rushing bars and fractured type for a fragmented mind. [1][2]
- Analogue texture is a style choice, not a default. Cooper built Se7en largely by hand. For a code-rendered film, add grain or hand-made marks only when the film's own research calls for them. [3][4]
- Set the default on-screen text budget at 150 words per minute or less, about 0.4 seconds per word (arithmetic by this digest's author). This sits inside DCMP's middle-to-upper targets (140 to 160) and below the BBC's secondary 160 to 180. [8][9][10]
- Convert between units with the convention stated: characters per second = words per minute x characters per word / 60. The Szarkowska paper uses five characters per word, so 150 wpm is about 12.5 cps and 17 cps is about 204 wpm (arithmetic by this digest's author). [9]
- Keep each on-screen text event on screen for at least 5/6 of a second and no more than 7 seconds. Split longer copy into several events. Netflix's general rules state both limits. [7]
- Keep text to two lines. Keep each line within 68 percent of the frame width at 16:9 (BBC, secondary) and break at punctuation. [10][7]
- Do not present more than a sentence one word at a time. For anything the viewer must understand, show phrases, not single words. [11]
- Treat kinetic-type speed as a hypothesis, not a settled number. The evidence is thin, so test two speeds on an animatic with a short viewer read before fixing one. [30][31]
- Design every text-bearing shot to work with the sound off. Publisher-reported figures from 2016 put silent social views high, so this is a sensible default, not a benchmark. [28]
- Flash safety: at most three general or red flashes in any one-second window. A general flash is an opposing luminance change of 10 percent or more, with the darker state below 0.80. A saturated-red transition counts as a red flash. This matches the project's three-per-second rule. [29]
- Keep all essential picture and every line of text inside the action-safe and graphics-safe areas defined by EBU R 95, and check the margins in the PDF before fixing the layout. [32]
- Settle the decision owner and the criteria before the pitch. Frame the sign-off around the problem, and use small trials (Design Council's Deliver phase) to reject weak options before the director sees them. [19][23]
- Write the picture before the words: the shot list and the emotional arc come first, and dialogue or narration is a last layer. Murch's emotion-first order supports this. [16][14]

## Open questions

- **Netflix English reading speed (not settled).** The General Requirements page defers to the language guides and gives no characters-per-second figure. Third-party presets give 20 cps for adult English and 17 for children's, with 42 characters per line; a research excerpt gives 17 cps for English. The English (USA) guide itself was not retrieved. Until it is checked, the skill should default to 17 cps as the conservative figure, which is a default choice and not a sourced fact.
- **BBC figures are secondary.** bbc.co.uk refused the fetch; the BBC figures come from Clevercast's summary. Verify against the current BBC guide before quoting them.
- **EBU R 95 margins are unverified.** The PDF could not be read; the 3.5 and 5 percent margins were seen only in a search excerpt.
- **Murch percentages beyond emotion are unverified.** Only the 51 percent emotion weight appeared in a fetched source; the others came from secondary write-ups.
- **Kinetic-type speed has no settled rate.** The Dolić 2024 findings could not be extracted, and the 2020 eye-tracking study reports no values in its abstract.
- **Logline template.** The "When..." formula some guides attribute to Snyder was not confirmed. The Save the Cat homepage has no logline formula.
- **Music-video treatment length and sections.** No authoritative standard was found; the sources are blogs and a pitch-template seller.
- **Crazy Eights wording.** The exact rules were not checked against the Sprint book.
- **Social-platform safe zones and LinkedIn video specs.** Reels safe-zone numbers seen in search (14 percent top, 35 percent bottom) are secondhand, conflict with other sources, and are not used. LinkedIn's video specifications were not found.
- **TikTok and sound-on usage.** Sources conflict on whether TikTok is sound-first or muted-first; not resolved.
- **Se7en brief detail.** The Fincher brief to Cooper came only from a fan-run blog, so it is not used.
- **Rascaroli chapter text.** Read only from a library record and abstracts.
